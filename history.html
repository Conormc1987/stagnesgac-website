Our Story

History of St Agnes GAC.

From the early days of Andersonstown to Páirc Naomh Úna — the people, places and moments that shaped the Aggies.

Our History

75 years, told through the moments that mattered.

Rather than one long history, we have broken the story of St Agnes into the people, places and milestones that built the club. It is a story of volunteers, families, Gaelic games and determination across generations.

1949–1951

The beginning of the Aggies

St Agnes chapel opened in 1949, when Andersonstown was still very different from the area we know today. The GAA club emerged soon afterwards, with its earliest meetings taking place in local homes.

Among those involved in the formative years were the McGrogan family, Dan Cashman, Eddie Armstrong, Albert and Séamus Owens and Paddy O'Hara. The club that became Naomh Úna was built from the commitment of local people.

1950s

The early days

Young players learned Gaelic football on Commedagh field at the top of Dunmisk Park. By 1954/55 the young Aggies had won the Antrim Minor League and had six players on the Antrim County Minor panel.

Families became the backbone of the club. Names including Brennan, Corr, Murphy, Gough, Bradley, Burke, Compston, McLarnon, Carroll, Turley, Mulgrew and Caldwell became part of the Aggies story.

1950s–1970

Finding places to call home

An early clubhouse was created in McCormick's former garage and workshop near what is now the back of the Fairdeal Garden Centre. The club later used lock-up garages beside the Shaw's Road pitches and then clubrooms above the shops beside Casement Park.

Those clubrooms were vacated in 1970, beginning a long period in which St Agnes had no permanent premises of its own.

Around 1980

The story of our crest

The club created a crest for its jerseys and kit around 1980. The design brought together the meaning behind Agnes and the Irish name Úna, with the lamb becoming its central symbol.

A cross was included as a reference to the church. The earliest version had a circle around the lamb and cross; this was later removed, leaving the familiar crest carried by generations of Aggies.

1973–1986

How Páirc Naomh Úna became our home

Woodlands Playing Fields has a remarkable history. Until July 1973, the site marked the final destination of the Belfast Twelfth of July Orange Parade and was known simply as “The Field”. After the parade moved to Edenderry, a new chapter began for Finaghy Road North.

The playing fields were developed under the Belfast Education and Library Board. Despite the lack of provision for Gaelic games, St Agnes persistently lobbied for access and eventually secured the use of a shared pitch.

In the early 1980s, plans were made to transfer Woodlands to Belfast City Council on 1 April 1986. Political events surrounding the Anglo-Irish Agreement disrupted council business and the transfer could not be completed. Six weeks after BELB locked the gates and walked away, St Agnes walked in.

The club installed goalposts, arranged maintenance and marked the pitch. In early May 1986, Páirc Naomh Úna was born — a defining moment in the history of the Aggies.

1986 onwards

Building Páirc Naomh Úna

Over the years new goalposts were erected, perimeter fencing installed, catch nets added and storage facilities brought on site. Extensive ground works significantly improved the playing surface.

What had once been “The Field” became a home for Gaelic games, used by generations of young hurlers and footballers. For today's players, it is simply their GAA home.

1999

A clubhouse of our own

After more than four decades without a permanent club venue or pitch, Naomh Úna took ownership of a former NHS clinic in South Link. The premises were officially opened in November 1999 by Ulster GAA Secretary Danny Murphy.

The new home included changing rooms, a committee room, kitchen and juvenile play area. Funding came from bodies including Making Belfast Work, the National Lottery, the Sports Council and Children in Need.

Today

Carrying the story forward

St Agnes has always been shaped by people who gave their time without expecting recognition — players, coaches, committee members, parents, families and volunteers. From handwritten match notices and bicycles in the early days to today's teams and juvenile structures, that community spirit remains the same.

As the club looks towards improved facilities and the generations still to come, Páirc Naomh Úna and St Agnes remain a home for Gaelic games in Andersonstown. We got there in the end — and the story continues.
